# Golden Retriever Arthritis

</div>

A Golden Retriever who is slow to stand is often sore in a joint, and the useful plan starts with a veterinary exam and a lean body, not a new chew. [VCA Animal Hospitals](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/arthritis-in-dogs) estimates that osteoarthritis affects at least 20 percent of dogs over one year old and 80 percent of dogs over eight. Those figures are for dogs in general. VCA also says simple aging is not the cause. The disease is inflammation and degeneration in one or more joints, sometimes called degenerative joint disease, and it interferes with ordinary movement.

The [American College of Veterinary Surgeons](https://www.acvs.org/small-animal/osteoarthritis-in-dogs/) describes what is breaking down: cartilage, the joint capsule, and the bone underneath. As cartilage wears away, the bone loses its cushion. The lining of the joint becomes inflamed and then scarred, and the joint fluid turns thin. Pain comes from that cartilage damage, exposed bone, friction, and inflammation. In many dogs the trigger is an earlier orthopedic problem, such as a cruciate ligament tear, osteochondrosis, or trauma. In others there is no single injury, and the joint wears from daily use. Hips, elbows, shoulders, knees, wrists, and ankles are the joints ACVS names. Young large-breed dogs with a developmental joint disease, and older overweight large-breed dogs, are the two groups ACVS sees most often. A Golden can be either.

Hip dysplasia is one developmental disease that feeds this. The malformed hip is its own problem, covered in the [hip dysplasia guide](/guides/golden-retriever-hip-dysplasia). The stiffness of later life, in that hip or in another joint, is this page. An older Golden's wider checkup is in the [senior care guide](/guides/golden-retriever-senior-care-guide).

Call your veterinarian, or a pet poison hotline, if any of them has already been given. The other miss is stopping all exercise. ACVS says high-impact running and jumping should be limited, and that controlled leash walks build the muscle that steadies the joint. A dog who only lies down loses that muscle and gets stiffer.</div>

## Read the stiffness in front of you

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Stiffness that eases after a few minutes is a clue. A leg that stays up, or a joint that is hot and swollen, is an appointment today.</div>

Dogs hide pain. VCA's sign list is about what changes in the house, and a dog does not have to show every item. Difficulty getting up and down. Walking slowly, beside you or behind you instead of out front. A joint that looks enlarged. Extra calluses over pressure points. A slight hunch in the spine. Weight shifted onto one side, or onto the front or the back. A limp. Hesitation on steps, or before a jump onto furniture. Pulling away when a hip or elbow is touched. Walks that get shorter. Unexpected snapping at people or other dogs. Even the coat can change, with hair that newly stands off or lies flat.

The [AVMA's senior-pet page](https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/pet-owners/petcare/senior-pets) uses the same pattern: less running, jumping, stair climbing, and car entry; favoring a leg; a stiff walk; trouble sitting or standing; more sleep; less play; resisting petting; unusual aggression. Any of those is a reason for an exam, because other diseases can look the same.

ACVS adds the gait clues that point at the hips and knees: a stiff front or back leg, a waddle or a sway through the body, bunny hopping, sitting with a leg stuck out or on one hip, and less interest in getting up. The [Merck Veterinary Manual](https://www.merckvetmanual.com/dog-owners/bone-joint-and-muscle-disorders-of-dogs/osteoarthritis-degenerative-joint-disease) pairs lameness with joint swelling, muscle wasting, and a joint lining that has thickened and scarred. Muscle loss is easy to miss under a Golden's coat. Compare the thighs.

## What the veterinarian is sorting

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">The diagnosis is an exam of the joints, plus X-rays when the veterinarian needs to see bone. You cannot score the stage from the couch.</div>

VCA diagnoses osteoarthritis with a physical exam, palpation to find which joint hurts and how much, and imaging, usually radiographs. ACVS expects pain when the joint is moved, grinding through the range of motion, a joint that will not flex or extend fully, swelling, and a stiff gait. Radiographs can show bone spurs, mineral where a ligament attaches, fluid in the joint, hardening of the bone under the cartilage, and cysts. ACVS calls arthroscopy, a camera inside the joint, the reference standard for looking at cartilage itself. Most Goldens never need that. They need the exam and the X-rays.

The [AVMA has described](https://www.avma.org/javma-news/2021-01-01/getting-ahead-osteoarthritis-pets) a staging tool, COAST, that runs from 0 to 4: a clinically normal dog with no risk factors, a normal dog who has risk factors, then mild, moderate, and severe osteoarthritis. The grade uses the owner's observations, the veterinarian's look at posture and motion, and the joint on manipulation, range of motion, and X-ray. That number is assigned in the clinic. A dog who looks symmetrical and still limps can have serious disease on both sides. Merck's clinical chapter notes that lameness is sometimes quieter than the X-rays, especially when both legs hurt.

Other causes have to come off the list. Merck's owner page includes trauma, infection, immune disease, and a joint that formed wrong. ACVS puts cruciate rupture, osteochondrosis, and trauma at the top of the causes that lead to osteoarthritis. Elbow dysplasia and hip dysplasia change how weight crosses the joint and start the wear early. If the hip is the story, stay with the hip dysplasia guide for that joint's surgery and breeding questions. This page is the pain and the daily life once the cartilage is the problem.

## Weight is the part you can change

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">ACVS calls a slender body the most important part of osteoarthritis care. Extra fat feeds inflammation and loads the joint.</div>

ACVS is blunt: a slender body weight is by far the most critical part of managing osteoarthritis. Fat produces inflammatory mediators that keep the inflammation going, and extra pounds put more force through the joint. Diet restriction in dogs has been shown to lower the rate of osteoarthritis in the hips, elbows, and shoulders. VCA says excess weight and obesity are highly correlated with the disease, and that normalizing body condition is critical. The veterinarian may prescribe a specific diet. Follow that plan rather than a calorie number from a forum.

The [2022 AAHA pain management guidelines](https://www.aaha.org/resources/2022-aaha-pain-management-guidelines-for-dogs-and-cats/nonpharmacologic-modalities-for-pain-management/) put weight in the same place. Fat tissue releases cytokines that circulate and contribute to osteoarthritis and to the way pain gets amplified. Lifetime studies in dogs support staying lean as a way to slow osteoarthritis and extend life. AAHA also lists weight management among the first-line steps, alongside the anti-inflammatory the veterinarian chooses, omega-3s, movement, and changes to the house.

The [weight guide](/guides/golden-retriever-weight-obesity) is where the body-condition check and the practical cutting-back live. The [feeding chart](/guides/golden-retriever-feeding-chart) is the baseline for how much food a Golden this size usually eats. For a dog who is already sore, the veterinarian sets the target and the pace. Do not crash-diet a dog who is also losing muscle.

VCA's prevention note belongs here too. The most effective way to prevent osteoarthritis is slow growth as a puppy and a lean body from growth into adulthood. Puppies raised on slippery floors have more hip dysplasia and osteoarthritis, ACVS says, and diets high in fat and protein during development have been tied to elbow osteoarthritis. The [puppy care guide](/guides/puppy-care) is the page for that age. VCA also says delaying spay or neuter until a dog is skeletally mature, which depends on the breed, can reduce some developmental bone problems. ACVS reports that early spay or neuter has been shown to raise the rate of those developmental diseases and, with them, earlier osteoarthritis. The timing is a decision with your veterinarian. It is not a promise that a later surgery prevents every sore hip.

## The medicines are the veterinarian's

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Prescription anti-inflammatories are the usual first drug. The drug and the amount belong to the clinic. Human painkillers stay in the cabinet.</div>

VCA says nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are generally the first treatment for osteoarthritis pain. The names on that page include meloxicam, carprofen, deracoxib, grapiprant, robenacoxib, and firocoxib. Your veterinarian decides whether your Golden is a candidate. This page does not give amounts. Merck says long-term use can sometimes cause loss of appetite, vomiting, and inflammation of the stomach and intestines. That is why a dog who goes off food or starts vomiting needs a call before the next dose.

In rare cases VCA says a corticosteroid is used instead of one of those anti-inflammatories. The two classes cannot be given together. Do not add a leftover steroid, or a human painkiller, to a dog who is already on a prescription anti-inflammatory.

Other drugs exist, and they are still the clinic's choice. VCA describes gabapentin as a medicine for chronic pain that can complement an anti-inflammatory, especially in an older dog who cannot take one. Bedinvetmab (Librela) is a monthly injection, a dog-specific antibody against nerve growth factor, which is elevated in arthritic joints and contributes to the pain. ACVS describes the same monthly injection. Some veterinarians use an injectable polysulfated glycosaminoglycan. VCA's [joint-support page](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/joint-support-and-diseasemodifying-osteoarthritis-drugs-dmoads-in-dogs) describes that drug as reducing joint inflammation and supporting cartilage and joint fluid. ACVS says more research is still needed on how much benefit those disease-modifying injections provide. Joint injections of hyaluronic acid, platelet-rich plasma, or a steroid are also clinic procedures. ACVS says their benefit still needs more study.

For the worst joints, surgery can remove the painful parts or replace them. VCA names total hip replacement, femoral head and neck ostectomy, and fusion of a joint, and says elbow or knee replacement is uncommon. ACVS adds repair of a torn cruciate ligament when that tear is the cause. The hip operations are discussed in the hip dysplasia guide. Whether any of them fits this dog is a surgeon's conversation, after the X-rays.

VCA's outlook, once the plan is in place, is that dogs with osteoarthritis can live a normal lifespan. ACVS says medical care lets most dogs live a long, active life, and that surgery aimed at the original cause can be curative in some joints. Merck says the outlook depends on which joint it is and how severe the damage has become.

## Supplements, and what the evidence actually supports

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Omega-3s are the supplement AAHA groups with first-line care. Glucosamine and chondroitin are commonly used, and AAHA does not treat them as proven pain relief.</div>

AAHA's 2022 guidelines put omega-3 fatty acids, from a supplement or from a therapeutic diet, in the first tier of chronic-pain care, next to weight, movement, changes to the house, and the anti-inflammatory the veterinarian prescribes. The guidelines say measurable pain relief has been associated with diets enriched in omega-3s. The same document places other nutritional supplements in a later tier and says there is no good evidence that non-omega-3 supplements relieve pain. ACVS still lists chondroitin, glucosamine, and omega-3s as the joint supplements most often recommended, and says the way they might ease pain is poorly understood. VCA's advice is to ask the veterinarian what data supports any nutraceutical before you buy it.

Merck's clinical chapter reports that eicosapentaenoic acid, an omega-3, and diets rich in it have improved gait and mobility and can have an effect that lets some dogs need less anti-inflammatory medicine. That change in medicine is the veterinarian's. Do not cut a prescription because a bottle of fish oil arrived.

## Movement that helps, and movement that flares

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Keep the dog moving on purpose. Trade jumps and long runs for regular leash walks, and add water when the joints will tolerate it.</div>

ACVS wants high-impact running and jumping limited, because they stir up more inflammation, and wants them replaced with controlled leash walks. Consistent low-impact work builds muscle around the joint and steadies it. Rehabilitation, range-of-motion work, and water, either an underwater treadmill or swimming, can improve mobility, muscle, and endurance. ACVS notes that acupuncture and laser are often recommended and that studies proving their benefit are still thin. AAHA's mobility guidance splits the walking by stage: moderate exercise daily in early osteoarthritis, and shorter sessions a few times a day later, with swimming or an underwater treadmill added under someone qualified. A sudden weekend of dock diving, after a quiet month, is how a sound dog picks up the injury that becomes osteoarthritis later.

Merck adds two practical details: exercise on soft surfaces, and warm compresses on the sore joint. Grass and dirt are kinder than a long pavement run. The [swimming guide](/guides/golden-retriever-swimming-guide) covers water for a Golden who already likes it. A dog who panics in a pool is not a rehab patient yet. Ask the clinic before you start a formal program.

The AVMA news piece on osteoarthritis describes the four pillars used by one of the veterinarians who helped build the staging tool: an effective pain medicine such as a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory, weight, diet, and exercise. They depend on each other. Pain control lets the dog walk. Walking helps the weight. Weight and walking ease pain. Your own veterinarian sets that sequence. Do not copy a feeding cut or a drug change out of a news article.

## The house

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">VCA's home list is short: a soft bed, dishes at elbow height, floors that do not skate, and a ramp so the dog stops jumping.</div>

That list is the whole environmental plan. The AVMA's senior page names the same tools: an orthopedic bed, a raised feeding platform, and pet stairs or a ramp. AAHA treats changes to the house as first-line pain care, including a floor and a bed the dog can use, and a way to get outside to toilet without a struggle. Cold, slick tile is where a Golden with weak hind legs falls. A runner in the hallway is the simplest version of a non-skid floor. Short nails, and a trim of the fur between the pads, help that foot meet the floor. The [paw care guide](/guides/golden-retriever-paw-care-guide) covers the trim. Raised bowls at elbow height, which is what VCA specifies, spare a sore neck and a sore back during meals.

## When to see a vet

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">New stiffness gets an appointment. A dog who will not use a leg, or who has been given a human painkiller, gets a call now.</div>

Book a regular visit for stiffness after rest, a shorter stride, hesitation on stairs, less play, or a dog who has started to guard a hip. The AVMA treats those activity changes as a reason to find the cause, because arthritis is common and it is not the only explanation. Bring the film and the food log. An older Golden should already be on the senior-care schedule. Arthritis is one reason that schedule matters.

Go the same day for a leg that will not bear weight, crying when the dog moves or is touched, a joint that becomes hot or suddenly swollen, a limp that appeared after a fall or a hard play session, or a back leg held up in a way that could be a knee. Those can be a flare. They can also be a cruciate tear, a fracture, or an infection. Do not wait them out overnight.

Call before the next dose if a dog on a prescribed anti-inflammatory stops eating or vomits. Call immediately if anyone in the house gave ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin, or acetaminophen. Tell the clinic the drug name, how much went in, and when. Do not start a second medicine while you wait.

<div className="my-8 rounded-lg border border-amber-200 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4">
  <div className="text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-wide text-amber-800">Tip</div>

## Questions owners ask

### What are the first signs of arthritis in a Golden Retriever?

VCA's common signs are trouble getting up and down, walking more slowly, a limp, hesitation on steps or before a jump, walks that get shorter, and a dog who does not want to be touched in one spot. The AVMA adds more sleep, less play, and irritability. The signs come and go. A good afternoon does not rule the disease out. Aging by itself is not the cause, VCA says, even though the problem is very common in dogs over eight.

### How is arthritis different from hip dysplasia?

Hip dysplasia is a joint that did not form correctly while the dog grew. Osteoarthritis is the inflammation and breakdown of cartilage and the tissues around a joint, and it can follow dysplasia or start from a cruciate tear, another injury, or ordinary wear. Many Goldens with hip dysplasia do develop osteoarthritis in that hip. A bunny hop or sitting on one hip can be either. The hip dysplasia guide covers the developmental joint. This page covers the sore joint's daily care.

### Will weight loss help a Golden who already has arthritis?

Yes. ACVS calls a slender body the most important part of care, because fat drives inflammation and extra weight loads the joint. VCA says normalizing body condition is critical, and the veterinarian may prescribe the diet. AAHA cites lifetime studies in which staying lean slowed osteoarthritis and lengthened life. The weight guide is the practical page. The amount of food for a dog who is already painful is still the clinic's plan.

### What exercise is safe?

Leash walks on soft ground, kept regular. ACVS wants running and jumping limited, and muscle maintained. AAHA suggests moderate daily exercise early, and shorter sessions several times a day later, with swimming or an underwater treadmill if a qualified person is guiding it. Merck likes soft surfaces and warm compresses. A long hike on a good day is how the next day gets worse. The swimming guide is the water half, for a Golden who is calm in water.

### Do joint supplements work?

Omega-3 fatty acids are the ones with a real place in the guidelines. AAHA puts them in first-line care and says omega-3-enriched diets have been associated with measurable pain relief. Merck's clinical chapter reports better gait with EPA. Glucosamine and chondroitin are widely sold. ACVS says they are commonly recommended and that the mechanism is poorly understood. AAHA says the evidence does not support a pain-relieving effect from non-omega-3 supplements. Ask the veterinarian before you start either, and do not treat the chart on the bottle as the amount to give.

### Can I give my Golden ibuprofen or another human painkiller?

No. VCA says not to reach into your own medicine cabinet for a limp or for diagnosed osteoarthritis. Human ibuprofen, naproxen, and acetaminophen are not a stand-in for a prescription anti-inflammatory. If one has already been given, call the veterinarian or a pet poison hotline and tell them what and when. Do not add a steroid to a dog who is already on a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory. VCA says those two classes cannot be used together.

### Can arthritis be cured?

The osteoarthritis itself is managed, not erased. VCA says there is no single treatment and no cure, and that dogs on an appropriate plan can still live a normal lifespan. ACVS says medical care lets most dogs stay active for a long life, and that surgery for the original problem, such as a cruciate repair or a joint replacement, can be curative in some cases. Which of those applies is the veterinarian's call after the exam and the X-rays.

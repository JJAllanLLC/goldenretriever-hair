# Golden Retriever Hip Dysplasia

</div>

Hip dysplasia is a hip that grew loose, and in a Golden Retriever the useful plan is an early check, a lean body, and steady low-impact exercise. The [American College of Veterinary Surgeons](https://www.acvs.org/small-animal/canine-hip-dysplasia/) describes it as a condition that begins while a dog grows and leaves the ball and socket of the hip with a loose fit. Each step moves the ball where it should not go. Over time that wears the cartilage, scars the tissue around the joint, and grows bone spurs. That later stage is hip arthritis.

[VCA Animal Hospitals](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/hip-dysplasia-in-dogs) calls it a genetic disease shaped by diet, environment, exercise, growth rate, muscle mass, and hormones, and names golden retrievers among the breeds where it is most often seen. ACVS says heredity is the biggest single risk factor, and that rapid weight gain from too much food can make it worse. How common it is in Goldens depends on who is counted. The [lifespan guide](/guides/golden-retriever-lifespan) walks through the OFA figure, the research figure, and why they differ.

This page is the developmental hip: what it looks like, how it is tested, and what can be done at each age. Once the hip has become a sore, arthritic joint, the day-to-day care is in the [arthritis guide](/guides/golden-retriever-arthritis). An older Golden's wider checkup is in the [senior care guide](/guides/golden-retriever-senior-care-guide).

## Read the signs in front of you

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">A bunny hop, a slow rise, or thin back thighs is a reason for an exam. The X-ray and the way the dog moves do not always match.</div>

ACVS lists the signs as limping, reluctance to rise or jump, weight shifted onto the front legs, muscle loss on the back legs, and hip pain. VCA adds weakness in the back legs, a wobbly walk, and reluctance to climb stairs. Owners often describe a bunny hop, with both back legs moving together at a run, or a Golden who sits with one hip rolled out.

The signs do not track the X-ray well. VCA says some dogs with marked changes on film act normal, while others with small changes are very lame. The [Orthopedic Foundation for Animals](https://ofa.org/diseases/hip-dysplasia/) says the same: some dysplastic dogs with severe arthritis run and play as if nothing is wrong, and no one can predict when, or whether, a dysplastic dog will start to limp. A dog who plays hard is not cleared, and a dog with an ugly film is not doomed.

ACVS sorts affected dogs into two groups. Young dogs with loose hips and no arthritis yet. Older dogs whose loose hips have turned into hip arthritis. The plan is different for each.

## How the hips are tested

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Diagnosis is a hands-on exam plus hip X-rays taken under sedation or anesthesia. A breeder's screening film and a puppy's diagnosis are related, but they are not the same test.</div>

VCA calls a hip X-ray under general anesthesia the preferred way to diagnose hip dysplasia, and says any dog suspected of it should be X-rayed as soon as possible. A loose hip the veterinarian can feel, called a positive Ortolani sign, also points to it. ACVS describes the Ortolani test as a palpation under light sedation that many general practice veterinarians can do at the 10 to 16 week vaccine visits. If the sign is there, the puppy will usually have hip arthritis by a year. If it is not there, the hip can still be loose.

ACVS calls PennHIP the most accurate X-ray method at an early age. It measures how far the ball can be moved out of the socket, and it needs a veterinarian with the training and equipment. Some general practice veterinarians and many board-certified surgeons offer it.

OFA grades hip films into seven categories. Excellent, good, and fair are within normal limits. Borderline is unclear. Mild, moderate, and severe are dysplastic. OFA offers preliminary films from 4 to 23 months, and its final grading is for adults. That is the screen most breeders use for their breeding dogs. ACVS points out that waiting until one or two years to look at a pet puppy's hips misses the window for the early surgeries. Both are true. One is a breeding screen. The other is about the puppy in your house.

VCA says genetic tests for hip dysplasia are still being researched and their validity is questionable. A DNA panel is not a substitute for hip films.

## Treatment depends on age

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Young dogs may be candidates for a surgery that changes how the hip grows. Adults usually start with weight, exercise, and prescribed pain relief. Severe hips may need a replacement.</div>

ACVS lays out the options by age. Your veterinarian, or a board-certified surgeon, decides which fit your dog.

**Juvenile pubic symphysiodesis (JPS).** A small surgery that closes a growth plate at the bottom of the pelvis so the socket covers more of the ball as the puppy grows. ACVS says it has to be done before 18 weeks of age, which is why the early check matters.

**Double or triple pelvic osteotomy (DPO or TPO).** The pelvis is cut in two or three places and rotated so the socket covers the ball better. ACVS says it fits young dogs, ideally under 8 to 10 months, with loose hips but no arthritis on X-ray. OFA says the procedure is not indicated once osteoarthritis is present.

**Total hip replacement (THR).** The ball and socket are replaced with implants. ACVS says it can be done from about a year of age and is one of the two surgical options with the most normal, pain-free function. OFA calls it the treatment of choice when an older dog's arthritis can no longer be managed with medicine, and names the high cost as its main drawback.

**Femoral head ostectomy (FHO).** The ball is removed and a false joint of scar tissue forms. ACVS says it can relieve pain enough that a dog under about 60 to 70 pounds may avoid daily anti-inflammatories, and that it does not restore normal hip function. OFA says long-term results are usually better in dogs under about 44 pounds. Many adult Goldens weigh more than that. Ask the surgeon how it applies to yours.

**Medical management.** OFA says that once arthritis shows on X-ray the changes cannot be reversed and usually progress. It also cites studies in which up to 76 percent of severely dysplastic dogs with arthritis lived comfortable lives with conservative care. ACVS lists what that care is: a very slim body, leash walks the dog tolerates, prescription anti-inflammatories monitored with blood tests, omega-3 fatty acids, and physical therapy. VCA says physical therapy is highly effective and should be part of any treatment plan. VCA also lists gabapentin, tramadol, and amantadine as drugs a veterinarian may add, and regular chondroprotectant injections such as Adequan for many painful dogs.

Do not give human pain medicine. VCA's [arthritis page](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/arthritis-in-dogs) warns against reaching into your own medicine cabinet for a limping dog. The drug, the amount, and the blood-test schedule belong to the clinic.

## Weight is the part you can change

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">ACVS says every dog with hip dysplasia should be kept very slim, from puppyhood to old age. It is the cheapest and most effective long-term care.</div>

ACVS is direct about it: keeping a dysplastic dog slim is the most effective, least expensive long-term way to keep that dog comfortable. OFA gives the check you can do at home. The ribs should be easy to feel, and there should be a waist in front of the hips.

Weight matters before the disease shows, too. VCA cites a study of puppies at risk for hip dysplasia. When they ate as much as they wanted, two thirds developed it. On measured meals, one third did. VCA also cites a study of German Shepherds in which overweight puppies were almost twice as likely to develop hip dysplasia. A Golden puppy should grow at a steady pace, not a fast one. The [growth chart](/guides/golden-retriever-growth-chart) and the [feeding chart](/guides/golden-retriever-feeding-chart) set the baseline. The [weight guide](/guides/golden-retriever-weight-obesity) covers the body-condition check and how to cut back.

VCA adds that spaying or neutering at an early age may raise the rate of hip dysplasia in some dogs. The timing is a decision to make with your veterinarian.

## Movement that helps, and movement that hurts

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Start with short leash walks and build up. Swimming keeps muscle on without pounding the joint. Jumping and sudden sprints are the ones to cut.</div>

OFA describes the method as trial and error. Start with short leash walks, add a little at a time, and scale back if limping or stiffness returns. The goal is muscle tone and fitness without pain afterward, because muscle around the hip helps steady a loose joint. VCA recommends moderate daily exercise and avoiding high-impact activity such as jumping.

OFA singles out swimming because the water carries the dog's weight. The [swimming guide](/guides/golden-retriever-swimming-guide) covers getting a Golden in and out of water safely. Soft ground beats pavement. A long weekend hike after a quiet week is how a comfortable dog ends up sore for three days.

ACVS says a dog with hip dysplasia cannot be highly athletic without surgery, and no medical program changes that. If you want a running, agility, or hunting partner, ask about surgery early rather than late.

## Joint supplements: what the evidence says

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Omega-3 fish oil has the best support. Glucosamine-type supplements are widely sold, but the evidence that they protect cartilage is weak. Ask your veterinarian first.</div>

ACVS includes fish-oil omega-3 fatty acids in medical management as an anti-inflammatory for the joints. VCA says most dogs with hip dysplasia should get veterinary-approved joint supplements, including omega-3s. The [2022 AAHA pain management guidelines](https://www.aaha.org/resources/2022-aaha-pain-management-guidelines-for-dogs-and-cats/nonpharmacologic-modalities-for-pain-management/) also cover weight, exercise, and rehabilitation as core parts of managing chronic joint pain.

The cartilage supplements are less certain. ACVS says there is no evidence in peer-reviewed literature that they repair or protect cartilage. OFA says oral nutraceuticals take about a month to reach useful levels and have few or no reported side effects. Some veterinarians still suggest them. Either way, a supplement does not replace weight control, exercise, or a prescribed medicine, and the chart on the bottle is not a dose for your dog.

## Set up the house

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Soft, warm bedding, traction on smooth floors, and a ramp instead of jumping. Small changes cut the daily strain on a loose hip.</div>

OFA says warmth helps arthritic hips, that the pain tends to be worse in cold, damp weather, and that a well-padded, warm bed helps. Be careful with heating pads: OFA warns they can burn the skin. The [AVMA's senior-pet page](https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/pet-owners/petcare/senior-pets) suggests orthopedic beds and ramps for older dogs with joint pain.

Slippery floors are a daily strain. Put washable non-slip runners on the paths your dog uses most, especially near the food bowl, the door, and the bed. Keep nails short so the paws grip. The [nail trimming guide](/guides/golden-retriever-nail-trimming-guide) shows how. Block stairs if they cause trouble, and use a ramp for the car instead of letting the dog jump.

## Buying a puppy: what clearances mean

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Ask to see the hip results for both parents. Clearances lower the odds. They do not guarantee sound hips.</div>

Because heredity is the biggest risk factor, breeding matters. OFA hip grades of excellent, good, or fair receive an OFA number, and that information is public for dogs with permanent identification, so you can look up the parents on the OFA site. PennHIP results are another way breeders show hip quality. Ask for both parents' results, not just one.

No result makes a puppy risk-free. VCA says diet, exercise, and growth rate still play a part. Marketing labels like "English Cream" do not promise better hips. The [English vs American guide](/guides/english-vs-american-golden-retriever) covers what those labels mean, and the [puppy care guide](/guides/puppy-care) covers the first months.

## When to see a vet

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">New stiffness, a bunny hop, or trouble with stairs gets an appointment. A leg that will not hold weight, or a dog who has been given a human painkiller, gets a call now.</div>

Book a regular visit for a puppy who bunny hops or sits on one hip, an adult who is slow to rise or skips the stairs, back thighs that look thin, or walks that keep getting shorter. Ask about a hip check at the puppy vaccine visits and again before spay or neuter, while the early surgeries are still possible.

Go the same day for a leg that suddenly will not bear weight, crying when the dog moves or is touched, a dog who cannot get up, or a limp after a fall. Those can be an injury rather than slow hip change.

Call before the next dose if a dog on a prescribed anti-inflammatory vomits, has diarrhea, or stops eating. Call right away if anyone gave a human painkiller, and tell the clinic what, how much, and when.

## Questions owners ask

### What are the first signs of hip dysplasia in a Golden Retriever?

ACVS lists limping, reluctance to rise or jump, weight shifted onto the front legs, thinner back thighs, and hip pain. VCA adds a wobbly walk and reluctance to climb stairs. Many owners first notice a bunny hop at a run. Many dogs show nothing at first, so a puppy who seems fine can still have loose hips.

### At what age does hip dysplasia show up?

VCA says signs can appear in puppies a few months old and are most common at one to two years, but that most dogs do not show signs until they are older, after years of gradual change. The looseness starts during growth even when the signs come later.

### Can hip dysplasia be prevented?

Not fully, because heredity is the biggest risk factor. You can lower the odds: choose a puppy whose parents have hip clearances, keep the puppy lean and growing at a steady pace, and talk with your veterinarian about spay or neuter timing. VCA cites a study in which free-fed at-risk puppies developed hip dysplasia twice as often as puppies on measured meals.

### Should a Golden with hip dysplasia still exercise?

Yes, in a controlled way. OFA recommends starting with short leash walks, building up gradually, and backing off if signs return, and it singles out swimming. Avoid jumping and high-impact play. Muscle around the hip helps hold it steady.

### Is surgery always needed?

No. OFA cites studies in which up to 76 percent of severely dysplastic dogs with arthritis lived comfortably with weight control, exercise, and medicine. Surgery is worth discussing early for young dogs, where JPS or a pelvic osteotomy can change how the hip develops, and later for dogs whose pain is no longer controlled, where a hip replacement or FHO may help.

### Do joint supplements help hip dysplasia?

Omega-3 fish oil has the best support, and ACVS and VCA both include it. ACVS says there is no peer-reviewed evidence that glucosamine-type cartilage supplements repair or protect cartilage. Ask your veterinarian before starting either.

### Can a Golden with hip dysplasia live a normal life?

Many do. OFA's figure for conservative care is encouraging, and ACVS says a hip replacement gives some of the most normal, pain-free function of the surgical options. A slim body, regular controlled exercise, and a plan from your veterinarian make the biggest difference.

### How is hip dysplasia different from arthritis?

Hip dysplasia is a hip that formed loose while the dog grew. Arthritis is the cartilage wear and inflammation that often follows. A dysplastic hip usually becomes an arthritic hip over time. The [arthritis guide](/guides/golden-retriever-arthritis) covers the daily care once the joint is sore.

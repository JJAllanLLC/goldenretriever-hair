# Golden Retriever Temperament: Personality, Traits and Behavior

</div>

The Golden Retriever's reputation is mostly earned. The [AKC breed standard](https://images.akc.org/pdf/breeds/standards/GoldenRetriever.pdf) sums up the temperament in three words: friendly, reliable, and trustworthy. [VCA Animal Hospitals' breed profile](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/dog-breeds/golden-retriever) describes a happy dog that is eager to please, outgoing toward strangers, and good with other pets. What the reputation leaves out is the work behind it. A Golden is a sporting dog bred to retrieve all day, and the calm family dog people picture is usually a grown, trained, well-exercised one.

So the honest answer has two parts. The breed gives you a friendly, people-focused, trainable starting point. Age, exercise, training, early experiences, and health decide how much of that you see on a given day. A large genetic study [reported by Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/dogs-personalities-arent-determined-by-their-breed/) found that breed explained only about 9 percent of the behavior differences between dogs. Read the traits below as what is typical, not as a promise about one dog.

## What the Golden Retriever temperament is like

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Friendly, eager to please, outgoing, and happiest with a job and a person nearby.</div>

A typical Golden Retriever is friendly, eager to please, and people-oriented. The AKC standard calls for a personality that is "eager, alert and self-confident" and a temperament that is "friendly, reliable, and trustworthy." It also spells out what is out of character for the breed: quarrelsomeness or hostility toward dogs or people in normal situations, or an unwarranted show of timidity or nervousness.

VCA's breed profile fills in the everyday picture:

- A playful and trustworthy companion for children
- Eager to please, but can be overly exuberant
- A happy dog, not easily bothered by things
- Outgoing toward strangers
- Gets along well with other pets and dogs
- Loves to retrieve, to the point of seeming compulsive, and loves to swim

Those last two lines matter more than they look. VCA's history of the breed says it was developed in the mid-1800s for strength to push through heavy cover, stamina to swim, toughness in cold water, and the gentleness to retrieve game unharmed. That drive to carry and fetch doesn't switch off at home, so give it a job: a retrieve, a toy to carry, a game.

## Golden Retrievers are good family dogs when someone has time for them

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Yes, Goldens are usually great with kids, as long as an adult supervises and trains both sides.</div>

Golden Retrievers are usually good with children. VCA rates the breed 5 out of 5 for being good with children. The [Golden Retriever Club of America (GRCA)](https://grca.org/find-a-golden/begin-the-search/is-a-golden-retriever-right-for-you/) says the breed has a well-deserved reputation as a wonderful family dog, then adds the condition: most Goldens are good with children only when the parents commit enough time and energy to raise them well.

The GRCA explains why families with young kids sometimes struggle. A new puppy takes about as much time as a toddler. Puppies naturally chase, jump, and bite, the way they did with their littermates, and children's noise and movement can set that off. The GRCA says success between puppies and children depends almost entirely on supervision by the parents.

In practice:

- Supervise every interaction between a puppy and young children.
- Teach kids not to climb on the dog, pull ears, or bother a resting dog.
- Give the puppy enough exercise and training that it isn't bringing pent-up energy to playtime.
- Keep the puppy with the family, not shut in a yard or garage, which the GRCA says makes manners worse.

A grown Golden is a big, strong dog, and the AKC standard puts males at 65 to 75 pounds. Even a gentle one can knock over a small child during an excited greeting, so manners matter as much as temperament.

## Goldens are friendly to strangers, not guard dogs

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">A Golden may bark when someone comes to the door, but it is far more likely to greet a visitor than guard against one.</div>

Golden Retrievers are not a guard-dog breed. The GRCA says adult Goldens are observant and some will bark at activity outside the home, but the typical Golden is friendly to everyone. In the club's words, a Golden "is more likely to offer to carry the intruder's flashlight than to physically protect the premises." VCA lists the breed as outgoing toward strangers and rates its barking 2 out of 5.

If you want a dog that barks when someone is at the door, some Goldens will do that. If you want a dog that protects, the breed's own club is telling you to look elsewhere.

## Aggression is uncommon in Goldens and usually has a cause

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Goldens aren't an aggressive breed, but any dog can growl or bite when it is in pain, afraid, or poorly socialized.</div>

Golden Retrievers are not known for aggression, and the breed standard treats hostility toward people or dogs as out of character. That doesn't mean a Golden can't bite. Breed is a weak predictor for any one dog, as the genetic study above found, and pain, fear, and a poor start in life can change how any dog behaves.

Pain is a cause worth ruling out first. VCA's page on [how pain affects behavior](https://vcahospitals.com/pediatric/puppy/behavior-training/how-pain-affects-behavior-in-dogs) says an increased tendency to growl or attempts to bite should be investigated as signs of pain. VCA's behavior specialists give the example of a dog that is fearful of children becoming more reactive, irritable, and aggressive as dental problems, arthritis, or an infection make it more uncomfortable. They say treating the medical problem, along with behavior modification, can resolve some of these cases.

Brief caution is not the same as aggression. A Golden that stares at a strange object, barks at a sudden noise, or backs away from something new is reacting to it. Repeated growling, lunging, snapping, or biting is different, especially if it is new. Start with a vet exam, then work with a qualified reward-based trainer or behavior professional. For the everyday problems, like jumping, barking, chewing, and digging, see the [behavior problems guide](/guides/golden-retriever-behavior-problems).

## Goldens usually get along with other dogs and pets

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Most Goldens are dog-friendly and pet-friendly, but introductions still need to be slow and supervised.</div>

Most Golden Retrievers do well with other dogs and household pets. VCA's breed profile says the breed gets along well with other pets and dogs, and the breed standard calls quarrelsomeness toward other dogs out of character.

Individual dogs still vary, as the genetic study found within every breed. Treat "good with other pets" as likely, not guaranteed. For a new dog or pet in the house:

- Introduce them slowly and keep the first meetings short and supervised.
- Feed separately and pick up high-value chews and toys until you know how they share.
- Keep small pets like rabbits, rodents, and birds safely housed, and supervise any time together.

## Goldens are easy to train because they want the reward

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Goldens learn fast with food, toys, and retrieving as rewards, and they learn bad habits just as fast.</div>

Yes, Golden Retrievers are easy to train. VCA rates the breed 5 out of 5 for ease of training and says Goldens do best with reward-based training that uses food or retrieving. It adds that the breed's aptitude for learning, happy attitude, and quick responses make it one of the top breeds for obedience competition. VCA also notes that Goldens are among the most successful guide and assistance dogs and work in search and rescue.

The catch is that a quick learner learns everything. VCA's behavior specialists explain that if a behavior is followed by something the dog wants, like food, attention, or affection, it is likely to be repeated, and that many unwanted behaviors are kept going by rewards owners give without meaning to. Letting a barking dog inside to stop the barking is their example. A Golden that gets petted for jumping up will keep jumping.

What works with this breed:

- Pay the behavior you want, with food, a toy, or a retrieve.
- Keep the rules the same for everyone in the house.
- Keep sessions short and frequent, especially for puppies.
- Skip harsh corrections. VCA says Goldens do best with reward-based training.

## Golden Retriever temperament changes with age

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Expect a mouthy puppy, a wild teenager, a steadier adult around two, and a slower senior.</div>

A Golden's temperament shifts a lot over its life, and most of the "this dog is a handful" stories come from the first two years. The GRCA says a Golden's need for exercise is most pronounced up to about two years of age, and that puppies and adolescents often have energy to burn. It also says Golden puppies mature at a rate of roughly one month of a puppy's life to one year of a child's life for the first 24 months, so an 8-week-old puppy is about like a two-year-old toddler.

| Puppy, about 8 weeks to 6 months | The GRCA says puppies naturally chase, jump, and bite, as they did with their littermates. Mentally, an 8-week-old is about like a toddler. | Gentle socialization, short training sessions, supervision, and confinement when no adult can watch, as the GRCA advises. |
| Adolescent, about 6 months to 2 years | Energy to burn, in a body that may already be big and strong. The GRCA says energy without an outlet can turn destructive and look "hyperactive." | Daily exercise before training, consistent rules, and patience. No jogging or biking before 12 months, per the GRCA. |
| Adult, about 2 to 7 years | The GRCA says most Goldens can adapt to a less active lifestyle once they are mature and trained. VCA says a Golden is a calm housedog if it gets enough exercise. | Regular vigorous exercise, which the GRCA says still helps adults and seniors stay lean and fit. |

## Exercise shapes the temperament you see at home

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">A Golden is a calm housedog only when it gets enough exercise, and it won't exercise itself.</div>

The calm Golden on the couch is usually a tired one. VCA's breed profile says the Golden makes a calm housedog, "but only if given adequate exercise," and that it needs daily walks, jogs, hikes, or play sessions, with swimming and retrieving as favorite exercise. It also lists obedience training and other mental games as important.

The GRCA adds two points owners often learn the hard way. First, Goldens are not self-exercisers, so a fenced yard by itself won't meet their needs. Second, an under-exercised young Golden channels that energy into behavior that is destructive and looks hyperactive. The GRCA's rule to remember: "A physically tired puppy is a well-behaved puppy!" The club also says jogging and biking are too hard on a young Golden's developing joints before at least 12 months of age, and to check with your vet first.

Mental work counts too, and VCA lists obedience training and other mental games as important for the breed. A retrieve, a scent game, a short training session, or a food puzzle gives that energy somewhere to go.

## Goldens are people dogs and don't do well left out

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Goldens want to be wherever their people are, and isolation shows up as barking, chewing, and digging.</div>

A Golden Retriever wants to be with its family. The GRCA calls this one of the breed's most endearing traits and says it means Goldens need to live as members of the family. It warns that Goldens kept away from people, in a fenced yard, a garage, or tied outside, become bored and stressed, and that this produces barking, chewing, digging, and hyperactivity.

That people focus is also why many Goldens follow you from room to room. It becomes a problem when the dog can't cope with being alone. The GRCA's guidance for puppies is that a tired, recently walked puppy with an empty bladder can usually nap through about 1 to 4 hours of confinement, but not a full workday in a crate. For longer absences it suggests day care or a dog walker, at least for the first several months.

## Breeding and early socialization set the starting point

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">A stable breeder and plenty of good experiences between about 3 and 14 weeks give a Golden its best start.</div>

Temperament starts before the puppy comes home. VCA's behavior specialists say genetic factors can influence or even dictate how a pet responds to things, and that the environment during the most sensitive periods of development has a major impact. The GRCA says serious hobby and competition breeders offer the most reliable health, temperament, and appearance, because they breed toward the AKC standard. Ask a breeder about the parents' temperaments, and meet them if you can.

Early socialization is the other half. The [AVMA](https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/animal-health-and-welfare/socialization-dogs-and-cats) says the best time to start is between 3 and 14 weeks of age for puppies, and that a broad range of positive experiences during this period can help prevent fearful, reactive, or aggressive responses later. (VCA's behavior page gives the socialization period as 3 to 12 weeks, so the exact cutoff varies by source. Both agree it is early.) The AVMA's advice:

- Ask the breeder or shelter what socialization the puppy has already had.
- Work with your veterinarian on a plan for gradual exposure to people, animals, places, and handling.
- Reward calm or curious reactions with praise, play, and treats, and let the puppy back away if it seems uncomfortable.
- Consider a well-run puppy class.
- Keep a puppy that isn't fully vaccinated away from animals of unknown vaccination status and places like dog parks.

The GRCA's list of things to include is practical: car rides, visitors, neighborhood walks, loud and unfamiliar places, baths, nail trims, ear cleaning, parks, and schoolyards. The [socialization guide](/guides/golden-retriever-socialization-guide) turns that into a practical order by age.

## When a temperament change is a vet question

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">A Golden that suddenly growls, hides, stops playing, or becomes clingy or anxious should see a vet before a trainer.</div>

A sudden change in a Golden's personality is often a health question first. VCA says behavior problems can be medical, behavioral, or both, and that a history, physical exam, and diagnostic tests help find any medical condition that is contributing.

VCA lists these behavior changes as possible signs of pain:

- Hiding or spending a lot of time alone when the dog is usually social
- A sudden drop in playfulness
- A hunched posture
- More growling or attempts to bite
- Pacing, whining, trouble being alone, or new destructive behavior
- Changes in appetite or in how the dog postures to pee or poop

In older Goldens, VCA describes cognitive dysfunction syndrome, a slowly progressive, age-related decline. Its signs include getting lost in familiar places, changes in how the dog interacts with familiar people, sleeping more by day and pacing at night, house soiling, forgetting known cues, less interest in play, and new fears or anxiety. VCA notes that arthritis, kidney disease, and other illnesses can cause similar changes, which is why a vet exam comes first.

### Are Golden Retrievers aggressive?

No, aggression is not typical of the breed, and the AKC standard treats hostility toward people or dogs as out of character. Any dog can growl or bite when it is in pain, afraid, or poorly socialized. VCA says more growling or attempts to bite should be investigated as possible signs of pain, so see your vet first.

### Are Golden Retrievers good with kids?

Usually, yes. VCA rates the breed 5 out of 5 with children. The GRCA says that holds when parents put in the time to exercise, train, and supervise the puppy, because puppies jump and mouth in play.

### Do Golden Retrievers bark a lot?

Not usually. VCA rates the breed 2 out of 5 for barking. The GRCA says some adults bark at activity outside, and that Goldens kept isolated from people tend to bark, chew, and dig more.

### Are Golden Retrievers easy to train?

Yes. VCA rates them 5 out of 5 for ease of training and says they do best with reward-based training using food or retrieving. They learn unwanted habits just as quickly if those habits get rewarded.

### When do Golden Retrievers calm down?

The GRCA says the need for exercise is most pronounced up to about two years of age, and that most Goldens can adapt to a less active life once they are mature and trained. Exercise and training make a big difference at any age.

### What words describe a Golden Retriever's personality?

The AKC breed standard uses "eager, alert and self-confident" for personality and "friendly, reliable, and trustworthy" for temperament. VCA adds happy, eager to please, outgoing, and sometimes overly exuberant.

# How to Obedience Train a Golden Retriever

</div>

This is the how-to page: the order to train in, how to run a session, how each cue gets taught and then made reliable, and what to do when it stalls. If you only want the list of cues a Golden should know, that is the [basic commands checklist](/guides/golden-retriever-basic-commands-checklist). Come back here when you are ready to teach them.

Goldens are food-motivated, people-focused, and mouthy, and they stay puppyish well past their first birthday. That makes them quick to learn a cue in the kitchen and slow to do it at the park. Most of this plan is about closing that gap.

## The plan at a glance

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Train in four stages. Do not move on until the current stage works on the first ask.</div>

Every cue goes through the same four stages. The stage tells you what to practice next, so you are never guessing.

## How to run a session

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">A few minutes, a few times a day, ending on a success.</div>

Short is the point. Golden puppies and adolescents lose focus fast, and a session that ends because the dog is bored teaches the dog to check out. Aim for two to five minutes, two or three times a day, plus quick one-rep moments during the day: a sit before the food bowl goes down, a down before the leash goes on.

1. **Pick one cue and one stage.** Write it down if it helps: &ldquo;Down, stage 2, kitchen.&rdquo;
2. **Load the pouch with tiny treats.** Pea-sized or smaller. A Golden will do more reps for many small treats than a few big ones.
3. **Start with an easy warm-up rep.** Something the dog already does well.
4. **Do five to ten reps of the new thing.** If the dog gets three wrong in a row, you raised the difficulty too fast. Go back a step.
5. **End on a rep the dog gets right,** then play or let the dog sniff.

VCA's guidance on reinforcement is that timing matters more than the treat: the reward has to land while the dog is still doing the behavior, or you pay whatever came next. That is why a marker word or a clicker helps. It tells the dog exactly which moment earned the food, and buys you a second to get the treat out.

## Teach each cue, step by step

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Lure or capture first, name it second, then stretch it.</div>

The checklist covers which cues matter. Here is how to actually teach the core five.

### Sit

1. Hold a treat at the dog&rsquo;s nose and move it slowly up and back over the head. As the nose goes up, the rear goes down.
2. The moment the rear touches the floor, say &ldquo;yes&rdquo; and feed.
3. After a few sessions where the dog sits as soon as your hand moves, say &ldquo;sit&rdquo; once, just as the rear starts to drop.
4. Then say &ldquo;sit&rdquo; with an empty hand, and pay from the pouch.

This is the luring method VCA describes. Do not push the hips down.

### Down

1. From a sit, take a treat from the nose straight down to the floor between the front paws, then slowly out along the floor.
2. Mark and feed the instant the elbows touch down. Feed on the floor so the dog stays down.
3. If the dog pops up, use a smaller movement, or capture it instead: keep treats handy and pay when the dog lies down on its own.
4. Add &ldquo;down&rdquo; once the dog folds as your hand starts to move.

Use &ldquo;off,&rdquo; not &ldquo;down,&rdquo; for getting off furniture or people. One word, one behavior.

### Stay

1. Ask for a sit or down. Wait one second, mark, and feed while the dog is still in position.
2. Build time in small steps: 2 seconds, 3, 5, 8. Mix in easy ones so it is not always harder.
3. Once the dog holds 10 seconds, add one step of distance, and drop the time back to a few seconds.
4. Always go back to the dog to pay. Then release with a word such as &ldquo;okay&rdquo; or &ldquo;free.&rdquo;

VCA&rsquo;s method is to delay the reward a little longer each time. The release word matters: the stay ends when you say it ends, not when the dog decides.

### Come

1. Indoors, take a few steps away, and say the dog&rsquo;s name in a happy voice. As the dog starts toward you, say &ldquo;come.&rdquo;
2. Pay big when the dog arrives, and touch the collar before you hand over the treat.
3. Play a back-and-forth game between two people in a hallway.
4. Only use the word when you are fairly sure the dog will come. Never call the dog to something it dislikes.

Outdoor recall on a long line is its own project. The [recall training guide](/guides/golden-retriever-recall-training-guide) covers it.

### Leave it

1. Hold a treat in a closed fist. Let the dog lick and paw. The moment the dog backs off, mark and pay from the other hand.
2. When the dog backs off right away, say &ldquo;leave it&rdquo; as you present the fist.
3. Progress to an open palm, then a treat on the floor under your foot, then a treat on the floor uncovered.

This one is worth extra time with a Golden, because the breed picks up everything.

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Expect weeks per cue, not days, and expect a dip during adolescence.</div>

These are realistic ranges for daily short sessions, not promises. Every dog is different, and an older dog with an old habit takes longer than a puppy with no habit yet.

## Adding distractions without losing the cue

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Change one thing at a time: distance, duration, or distraction.</div>

A Golden who sits perfectly in the kitchen and ignores you at the park has not been stubborn. The cue was learned in one place, and it has not been practiced anywhere else yet.

- **New rooms first.** Bedroom, hallway, garage.
- **Then the yard,** at a quiet time of day.
- **Then the front sidewalk,** then a quiet park, then a busier one.
- **Each time the place gets harder, make the ask easier.** Short stays, close distance, better treats.

If your Golden cannot take food, the environment is too much. Move farther away from the distraction until the dog can eat again, then train there.

VCA also warns against yelling cues: a loud voice raises arousal and makes it harder for the dog to respond calmly. Say the dog&rsquo;s name, give the cue once in a normal voice, and wait a beat.

## Why reward-based training, and what to avoid

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Reward what you want. Skip punishment-based tools.</div>

The American Animal Hospital Association&rsquo;s behavior management guidelines recommend reward-based training and advise against punishment-based methods. The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior&rsquo;s position statement on humane training says the same, and links aversive methods with fear, stress, and aggression. That is why this plan uses no leash pops, prong collars, choke chains, or shock collars.

Not paying for the wrong behavior is enough. If the dog jumps for the treat, the treat goes away. If the dog sits, it arrives.

## When to call your veterinarian or a behavior professional

<div className="takeaway my-6 rounded-r-lg border-l-4 border-amber-600 bg-amber-50 px-5 py-4 text-xl font-semibold text-amber-950">Training problems and health problems can look the same.</div>

A dog who suddenly stops sitting, will not lie down, snaps when touched, or seems slow or stiff may be in pain, and that is a veterinary question first. The AAHA guidelines treat behavior and medical health together for this reason. Talk to your veterinarian, and ask for a referral to a veterinary behaviorist or a certified reward-based trainer if your Golden shows fear, guarding, or aggression. The [behavior problems guide](/guides/golden-retriever-behavior-problems) is a starting point for those patterns.

For puppies, the AVMA notes that socialization and early training happen during a sensitive period, and your veterinarian should tell you which classes and outings are safe before vaccines are finished. The [socialization guide](/guides/golden-retriever-socialization-guide) covers that.
